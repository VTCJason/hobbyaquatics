Understanding AI

How AI Actually Works

You don't need a computer science degree to understand what's happening under the hood. Five concepts explain almost everything you'll encounter as a user of these tools.

Live Exercise — Fill in the Blank
  • 1 Three Little
  • 2 I Dream of
  • 3 Peter Piper Picked a
  • 4 The 12
  • 5 Good

Write your answers before the group shares. Notice which ones you filled in instantly — and which ones you hesitated on. That hesitation is the entire story of how AI works.

The Insight

Notice that #5 — "Good ___" — had several reasonable answers: Good Night, Good Morning, Good Day, Good enough. Without more context, any of them could be right. That uncertainty is exactly what an AI faces when your prompt doesn't give it enough to work with. More context doesn't just help — the right context is what separates a useful AI response from a generic one.

Five Things Worth Understanding

📍
1. Embeddings — The Map of Meaning
When you type a place into GPS, you think in words. Your phone thinks in coordinates. The conversion — human name to machine numbers — is exactly what an embedding is. AI converts words and sentences into numerical addresses on a map of meaning. Ideas that mean similar things get similar coordinates. That's why AI can understand "Why is the sky blue?" and "What makes the sky appear blue?" as the same question.
"Embeddings are how computers turn ideas into addresses."
🧩
2. Tokens — The LEGO Bricks of Language
AI doesn't read words. It reads tokens — chunks of language small enough to be counted and measured. "Running" might split into "run" + "ning." Tokens are the compromise between letters (too small to carry meaning) and whole words (too large and unpredictable). Think of them as LEGO bricks: a limited set of pieces that can build almost anything.
"Tokens are how flowing human language becomes something a computer can step through, one piece at a time."
🧠
3. Models — Compressed Pattern Memory
A model is not a database. It's not a brain. It's a compressed memory of patterns learned from billions of examples. Like learning to drive — you don't memorize every road, you learn how roads usually work. Bigger models handle more complex patterns and longer context. Smaller models are faster and better for specific tasks. Neither is always better.
"Models store what usually follows what."
🖼️
4. LLMs vs. Diffusion Models
LLMs (language models) predict the next token. They build forward, one piece at a time, using everything they've seen so far to choose what comes next. ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are LLMs.

Diffusion models start with noise and remove what doesn't belong — like a sculpture emerging from stone. Midjourney, Firefly, and DALL·E are diffusion models.
"Language models build forward. Diffusion models clean backward."
⚠️
5. Hallucinations — Guessing Is the Job
AI "hallucinations" are not lies. They're confident wrong guesses — the same mechanism that produces brilliant answers also occasionally produces plausible nonsense. The model is always choosing the most statistically likely next token. Sometimes it guesses wrong. It has no internal moment where it says "I don't know." It only says "this seems right."
"AI doesn't hallucinate because it's broken. It hallucinates because it's guessing — and guessing is the job. Verify anything that matters."
🎯
Context — Quality Beats Quantity
Context is what the model can see right now: your prompt, earlier messages, documents you've shared. More context helps — up to a point. Research shows that too much irrelevant context can actually degrade performance, as the model must filter out noise. The lesson: focused, relevant context outperforms long, rambling prompts.
"Context is what lets AI stay on the same page instead of starting over."

The Skill That Multiplies Everything: Prompting

Bad Prompt vs. Good Prompt

Bad: "Explain artificial intelligence."
Result: correct, generic, not useful to you specifically.

Good: "Explain artificial intelligence to a non-technical adult audience in a church Life Group. Avoid jargon. Use real-world analogies. Focus on what AI can and cannot do today. Keep it under 3 minutes of reading time."
Result: the model didn't get smarter — the probability space got smaller.

The four questions that make any prompt better: Who is this for? What do they already know? What do they need it for? What does success look like? Add those answers to your prompt, and the output improves dramatically.

AI is not intelligent like a person. It is competent like a system. It doesn't know why a metaphor works. It knows that it works. That's enough to be useful. It's not enough to be conscious.
— From a conversation on explaining AI to a non-technical audience

A Word on Discernment

"The skill that may matter most in the age of AI is the distinctly human one — learning to be a critical thinker, to question sources, to hold uncertainty without paralysis, and to act wisely in conditions we cannot fully calculate. That skill was valuable before AI. It is indispensable now."
— AI Voices Run Amuck Fact Assessment, April 2026

AI does not understand meaning the way we do. It understands relationship and closeness — pattern, proximity, and probability. It can produce answers that sound authoritative and are entirely wrong. Verify anything important. Trust, but check. These are not new skills — they are exactly the skills a person of faith already exercises when reading, evaluating, and weighing what is true.

The Alignment Distinction — Worth Knowing

Obedience: follows instructions literally.
Alignment: follows intent, values, and constraints.

Humans (ideally) are aligned. Machines are obedient by default. A powerful, obedient, insufficiently aligned system is not a helper — it's a force multiplier for whatever intent it's given. This is why the guardrails, values, and ethical frameworks built into AI systems matter — and why it's worth paying attention to how the companies building these tools think about that responsibility.

    Ask ChatGPT and Claude the exact same meaningful question — something you actually care about. A question about faith, a relationship challenge, a decision you're working through, a topic you want to understand better. Notice how each responds: the tone, the depth, the caveats, the areas where they differ. You'll immediately feel the difference in their personalities.

    Generate One Image Using the Prompt Formula
    Go to Google ImageFX (free, no subscription). Use the formula:
    Subject + Style + Composition + Lighting + Key Details + Constraints
    Try something personal — an image for a greeting card, a background for a presentation, an illustration of a Bible passage. Run it, then tweak one element and run it again. The revision is where the learning happens.
  • 3
    Transcribe Your Next Phone Call or Meeting
    Sign up for Fireflies.ai or Jamie (both free to start) and let them transcribe your next important call. Review the summary afterward. Notice what it captured, what it missed, and how it might change the way you take notes in the future.
Bonus — Teach Someone Else

Try the fill-in-the-blank exercise from today's presentation with someone at home — a family member, a friend, a neighbor. "Three Little ___, I Dream of ___, Good ___." Walk them through why "Good ___" has more than one right answer. You will understand tokens and context more deeply by explaining them than you did by hearing about them. That is also how AI works: the act of explaining shapes what is understood.
